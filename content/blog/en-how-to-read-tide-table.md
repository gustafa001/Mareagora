A tide table looks intimidating at first glance — a grid of numbers, abbreviations, and times that seems designed to confuse. But once you understand the logic behind it, reading a tide table becomes second nature. This complete guide will teach you everything you need to know, from the basic concepts of tidal movement to reading professional maritime tide predictions.

## What Is a Tide Table?

A tide table is a **published set of daily predictions** showing the times and heights of high and low tides at a specific location. These predictions are calculated from decades of tidal observations combined with astronomical data about the positions of the moon and sun.

Tide tables are published for a full calendar year by government hydrographic offices such as:
- **NOAA** (USA) — tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov
- **UK Hydrographic Office (UKHO)** — admiralty.co.uk
- **SHOM** (France) — shom.fr
- **Australian Bureau of Meteorology (BoM)** — bom.gov.au
- **Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA)** — jma.go.jp
- **Brazilian Navy (CHM)** — marinha.mil.br

Each tide table is location-specific. A table for Sydney will be different from one for Melbourne, even though they're in the same country.

## Key Tidal Terms You Must Know

Before reading any tide table, learn these fundamental terms:

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **High Water (HW)** | The highest point of a tide cycle; also called *high tide* or *preamar* (PT) |
| **Low Water (LW)** | The lowest point of a tide cycle; also called *low tide* or *baixamar* (PT) |
| **Tidal Range** | The vertical difference in height between high and low water |
| **Flood Tide** | The rising tide — water level is increasing |
| **Ebb Tide** | The falling tide — water level is decreasing |
| **Slack Water** | The brief period of minimal tidal current near high or low water |
| **Spring Tides** | The strongest tides, occurring near new and full moons |
| **Neap Tides** | The weakest tides, occurring near first and last quarter moons |
| **Chart Datum** | The reference level (zero) from which tidal heights are measured |
| **MLLW** | Mean Lower Low Water — the US standard datum |
| **LAT** | Lowest Astronomical Tide — the Australian/UK standard datum |

## Anatomy of a Tide Table: Column by Column

A typical tide table for a given day looks like this:

| Date | Time | Height | High/Low |
|---|---|---|---|
| 15 Jul 2026 | 03:42 | 4.8 m | HIGH |
| 15 Jul 2026 | 09:58 | 0.6 m | LOW |
| 15 Jul 2026 | 16:14 | 4.5 m | HIGH |
| 15 Jul 2026 | 22:27 | 0.9 m | LOW |

### Reading Each Column

**Date**: The day the prediction applies to. Always check this — tides shift by approximately 50 minutes each day.

**Time**: The predicted time of the high or low water event. Always check whether the table is in **local time** or **UTC/GMT**. During daylight saving time, you may need to add 1 hour.

**Height**: The predicted water level at that moment, measured in metres or feet above the **chart datum**. A height of 0.0 m means the water is at the lowest possible astronomical level (LAT datum). Negative heights, though rare, indicate the water is below the theoretical minimum.

**High/Low (H/L)**: Whether the event is a high or low tide. Some tables use arrows: ▲ for high, ▼ for low.

## Understanding Tidal Range

The tidal range is simply **High Water Height minus Low Water Height**. For the example above:
- First range: 4.8 m − 0.6 m = **4.2 m** (strong spring tide)
- Second range: 4.5 m − 0.9 m = **3.6 m**

A large range means dramatic changes in the coastline — beaches may appear and disappear, rocks emerge from the sea, and tidal currents are powerful.

## What Is the Tidal Coefficient?

Some European tide tables (particularly French and Portuguese ones) include a **tidal coefficient (coeficiente)** — a number from 20 to 120 indicating the strength of the tide:

| Coefficient | Type | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| 95–120 | Very strong spring | Maximum range, powerful currents |
| 70–95 | Spring | Large range, strong currents |
| 40–70 | Intermediate | Moderate range |
| 20–40 | Neap | Small range, weak currents |

## How Tides Work: The Moon and Sun

Tides are caused by the **gravitational pull of the moon** (and to a lesser extent, the sun) on Earth's oceans.

- **New Moon and Full Moon**: The sun, Earth, and moon are aligned. Their gravitational forces combine, producing **spring tides** with the largest ranges.
- **First and Last Quarter Moon**: The moon and sun are at right angles to Earth. Their gravitational forces partially cancel, producing **neap tides** with smaller ranges.
- **Perigee**: When the moon is closest to Earth in its elliptical orbit. Tides are exceptionally strong — called *King Tides* in Australia and New Zealand.

## Practical Examples: Planning Your Activity

### Going Surfing
Check the tide table and note whether the tide is **rising (flood)** or **falling (ebb)** at your planned surf time. Most surf breaks have a preferred tidal state — usually mid-tide on a flood for beach breaks, or lower tide for reef breaks where more water can cause the wave to not break cleanly.

### Going Fishing
Most species are most active during **tide changes** — the transition from ebb to flood or flood to ebb. Plan to be fishing in the **2 hours before and after** high or low water.

### Exploring Rock Pools
You want the lowest possible water. Look for days with a **spring low tide** (large range, low coefficient), and time your visit to arrive **30 minutes before the predicted low water** so you can watch the pools emerge.

### Boating and Kayaking
Avoid strong tidal currents by paddling during **slack water** — the 30–60 minute window around high or low water when currents are minimal. Always plan your return journey accounting for the tide direction.

## Common Mistakes When Reading Tide Tables

1. **Ignoring daylight saving time** — always verify whether the table is in local time or UTC.
2. **Using a table for the wrong location** — tides can be very different just 20 km apart.
3. **Confusing height with depth** — tidal height is measured from chart datum, not from the actual seabed depth.
4. **Ignoring weather effects** — strong onshore winds and low atmospheric pressure can raise actual sea levels above predicted values by 0.5 m or more.
