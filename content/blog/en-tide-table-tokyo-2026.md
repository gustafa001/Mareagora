Tokyo Bay (東京湾, *Tōkyō-wan*) is one of the world's busiest maritime environments — a semi-enclosed body of water flanked by the world's largest metropolitan area and Japan's most important commercial ports. Yet beneath the container ships and ferry routes, a constant tidal rhythm has shaped life on the bay for millennia. In 2026, understanding Tokyo's tides can unlock some of the best fishing, birdwatching, and coastal experiences the Kantō region has to offer.

## Overview of Tokyo Bay Tides

Tokyo Bay experiences **semidiurnal tides** — two high tides and two low tides every approximately 24 hours and 50 minutes. The tidal range at the Harumi tide gauge (central Tokyo Bay) averages around **1.3–1.5 metres** at spring tides and 0.5–0.8 m at neap tides.

The bay's semi-enclosed geometry means tides behave slightly differently across its 50 km length. Tidal data at **Yokohama** (the southern entrance) differs slightly from **Chiba** (the eastern shore) and the inner bay near Haneda Airport. The Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA) maintains a network of tide gauges across the bay for precise, location-specific predictions.

## How to Read the Tokyo Tide Table

Japanese tide tables are published by the **Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA)** and the **Japan Coast Guard Hydrographic and Oceanographic Department**. They reference **Tokyo Peil (T.P.)** — the standard elevation datum used throughout Japan, defined as mean sea level at the Eiraku-cho tide gauge in Osaka.

| Column | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **日付 (Date)** | Month/Day |
| **満潮 (Manche)** | High tide time and height |
| **干潮 (Kanchō)** | Low tide time and height |
| **Height (cm)** | Water level above or below T.P. |

### Japan Standard Time (JST)
All Japanese tide tables are published in **Japan Standard Time (JST, UTC+9)**. Japan does not observe daylight saving time, so no seasonal conversion is needed — a welcome simplicity for tide table readers.

## Spring and Neap Tides at Tokyo Bay

- **大潮 (Ōshio — Spring tides)**: Largest tidal ranges, coinciding with new and full moons. Currents in the bay are strongest, and tidal flats (干潟, *higata*) are most fully exposed.
- **小潮 (Koshio — Neap tides)**: Smaller range, calmer conditions. Favoured for leisure activities requiring stable water levels.

Tokyo's largest spring tides in 2026 will occur in **March and September**, when equinoctial alignment of the sun and moon produces the most powerful tidal forces.

## Best Times for Tokyo Bay Activities

### Sea Fishing (東京湾釣り — Tokyo-wan Tsuri)
Tokyo Bay is a legendary fishing destination among Japanese anglers. The tidal calendar is essential:

- **Seabass (スズキ, Suzuki)**: Most active around tide changes — especially the first 2 hours of an incoming tide under bridges and near river mouths. The **Arakawa River** and **Edogawa River** mouths are prime spots.
- **Flounder (ヒラメ, Hirame)**: Prefer the ebb tide on sandy flats. Best during spring low tides when flatfish concentrate in deeper channels.
- **Tokyo Bay Gizzard Shad (コハダ, Kohada)**: A beloved sushi ingredient, most abundant in shallow bays on flood tides.
- **Yellowtail (ブリ, Buri)**: Seasonal visitor (autumn/winter), caught at depth on any tide.

The golden rule among Tokyo anglers: *"The best fishing is the two hours before and after a tide change."*

### Birdwatching at Tidal Flats
Tokyo Bay retains some of Japan's most important tidal flats for migratory shorebirds:

- **Sanbanse (三番瀬)**: A protected tidal flat in Ichikawa City, Chiba, visited by tens of thousands of migratory waders each spring. **Best visited 1–2 hours before low tide**, when birds are most actively feeding as flats emerge.
- **Hakkei-jima area**: Shallow tidal areas accessible at low water, popular with telescopes and binoculars.

### Kayaking in Tokyo Bay
Kayaking in Tokyo Bay is possible but requires careful planning around tidal currents and commercial shipping. The **Zushi and Hayama** areas, south of Yokohama, offer more sheltered kayaking and are best accessed at **high to mid-tide** to avoid grounding on tidal flats.

## Curious Facts About Tokyo Bay's Tides

- Tokyo Bay is the site of **70% of Japan's reclaimed land**, meaning many of the bay's shores are artificial — built on areas that were once tidal flats or open water.
- The famous **Rainbow Bridge** (レインボーブリッジ) spanning Tokyo Harbour is designed to accommodate the bay's tidal range and typhoon-driven storm surges.
- **Typhoon surges** in Tokyo Bay are a serious hazard: when a powerful typhoon pushes water from the Pacific into the funnel-shaped bay, sea levels can rise by 3 metres above the predicted tide — as occurred devastatingly in the 1959 Isewan Typhoon.
- Tokyo's historic fishing village culture (漁村文化) was built entirely around the tidal calendar. Many traditional festivals and fishing methods remain tied to tidal cycles.
